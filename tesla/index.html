📜 Who Was Nikola Tesla?

👶 A Boy from Smiljan

Nikola Tesla was born in 1856 in the village of Smiljan, then part of the Austrian Empire and now in Croatia. His family was Serbian. His father was an Orthodox priest, and his mother was clever at inventing handy tools for the home.

📚 Student in Graz

He studied engineering in Graz, Austria. There he saw an electric machine that sparked a lot, and he began dreaming of a better way to make motors run.

🇺🇸 Off to America

In 1884, Tesla arrived in New York and worked for Thomas Edison's company for a few months. They disagreed, and Tesla left to work on his own ideas.

🌀 The Big Idea

Tesla figured out how to use alternating current (AC) to make a rotating magnetic field. That let him build a simple, tough induction motor. Motors like it still run fans, pumps, and factory machines today!

⚔️ The War of the Currents

In the late 1880s, Edison backed DC power. George Westinghouse bought rights to Tesla's AC patents and backed AC. The two sides competed fiercely over which system would power America.

🎡 AC Wins the Big Show

In 1893, Westinghouse's AC system lit up the Chicago World's Fair with many thousands of lights. Then a power plant at Niagara Falls used Tesla's AC system and, in 1896, sent power more than 20 miles to the city of Buffalo.

⚡ The Tesla Coil

Around 1891, Tesla invented the Tesla coil, a machine that makes very high voltages and long, crackling sparks. He used it to explore wireless signals and lighting.

📻 Radio: Many Inventors

Tesla worked on wireless radio signals in the 1890s, and so did Guglielmo Marconi and others. In 1943, the U.S. Supreme Court decided some of Marconi's radio patents were not valid, partly because others, including Tesla, had similar ideas first. Radio really had many inventors.

🗼 Wardenclyffe Tower

Around 1901, Tesla began building a giant tower at Wardenclyffe on Long Island, New York. He dreamed of sending messages, and even power, without wires around the world. The money ran out, it was never finished, and it was torn down in 1917.

🕯️ His Last Years

Tesla had brilliant ideas but was not good at making money from them. He died in 1943 in a hotel room in New York City, at age 86, with very little money.

🧲 A Unit Named for Him

Scientists measure the strength of a magnetic field in units called teslas, named in his honor. A fridge magnet is only about one hundredth of a tesla!

🔌 AC vs DC Explorer

Electric current is tiny particles called electrons moving through a wire. In DC (direct current) they flow one way. In AC (alternating current) they wiggle back and forth, over and over. Both can light a bulb!

🏭 ➜ 🏘️ Sending Power Far Away

Electricity loses energy as it travels through long wires. Tesla's AC could use transformers to raise the voltage very high for the long trip (losing much less energy), then lower it again near homes. Back then, DC couldn't easily do this. Slide the town farther away!

🌀 The Rotating Magnetic Field

Tesla's clever trick: put three coils of wire around a circle and feed each one AC that peaks at a different moment. Together they make a magnetic field that spins around, and it drags a metal rotor along with it. No sparking brushes needed!

⚡ Pretend Tesla Coil Spark Toy

⚠️ Real Tesla Coils Are Dangerous

Real Tesla coils make extremely high voltages. Only trained experts with special safety gear should ever run one. Never poke things into electrical outlets, and stay far away from power lines, especially fallen ones.

🕵️ Legend or Fact?

Tesla is so famous that lots of stories about him get exaggerated. Be a history detective: is each one a fact or a legend?

👪 For Grown-ups

What this teaches

  • Nikola Tesla's life (1856–1943) and his real contributions: the rotating magnetic field, the AC induction motor and polyphase system, the Tesla coil, and early wireless work.
  • Science: AC versus DC, why high-voltage AC with transformers made long-distance power practical, and how a rotating field turns a motor.
  • History skills: separating documented facts from popular legends.

Grade band

About grades 3–5. Read-aloud helps younger readers.

Accuracy notes

  • AC existed before Tesla. His key contribution was a practical AC motor and polyphase system (patents licensed by Westinghouse in 1888).
  • The often-told “$50,000 promise” from Edison comes from Tesla's own later account and can't be confirmed, so it's labeled a legend here.
  • The 1943 Supreme Court case (Marconi Wireless Telegraph Co. v. United States) invalidated parts of Marconi's patents, citing earlier work by Lodge, Tesla, and Stone. It did not declare Tesla “the inventor of radio,” and it was decided months after Tesla died.
  • Niagara: the Adams plant began generating in 1895, and power reached Buffalo (about 26 miles) in November 1896.
  • The animations are simplified teaching models: electron speed, line losses, and the spark toy are not to scale.

Safety

The lesson includes a clear warning about real Tesla coils, outlets, and power lines. The spark toy is purely on-screen.

Time

About 35–45 minutes; easy to split (life and AC/DC, then motor, spark toy, legends, and quiz).

Follow-up

Read the label on a phone charger together: it takes AC from the wall (100–240 V) and puts out low-voltage DC. That little box is a modern cousin of the AC vs DC story.
