🚀 Starship: Physics and a Mars Landing

How a giant reusable rocket is designed to work — and a game where you land on Mars

⭐ Start 🚀 Two Stages ⬆️ Thrust 🔥 Fuel 🛡️ Coming Back 🪐 Mars 🎮 Land on Mars ❓ Quiz 🏁 Badge 👪 Grown-ups

Can you land a giant rocket on Mars?

Starship is a huge reusable rocket being built by SpaceX. Engineers plan for it to carry people and cargo to Earth orbit, the Moon, and someday Mars. It has not landed on Mars yet — but you can practice the landing idea!

To fly, a rocket must push harder than it weighs. To come home, it must slow down in air, stay cool behind a heat shield, then flip and fire its engines for a soft landing.

Mars is trickier than Earth: gravity is weaker, but the air is much thinner. Let’s learn the physics, then try Land on Mars!

🚀 Two Stages, One Giant Vehicle

The whole vehicle is often called Starship, but it has two stages that stack together for launch.

🧱 Super Heavy — the booster

The bottom stage is called Super Heavy. It is the booster. In SpaceX’s current design it is about 72 meters tall and is powered by 33 Raptor engines. It pushes the stack off the pad, then is designed to fly back and be reused.

🚀 Starship — the ship

The top stage is the Starship spacecraft. It is about 52 meters tall and has 6 Raptor engines. After the booster lets go, the ship is designed to fly on to orbit — and, someday, much farther.

📏 Taller than a 30-story building

Stacked together, SpaceX’s published design is about 124 meters tall (about 407 feet) and 9 meters wide. That’s a flying building! It is designed to be fully reusable: both stages should come back and fly again.

⬆️ Thrust Versus Weight

⬇⬆ Newton’s Third Law

A rocket engine throws hot gas down. Because every push has an equal push the other way, the ship is pushed up. That upward push is called thrust.

⚖️ Beat your weight

On the launch pad, Earth’s gravity pulls the rocket down. That downward pull is its weight. To lift off, thrust must be greater than weight. If they are equal, it just hovers. If thrust is smaller, it stays on the ground — or falls.

🔥 Fuel and Raptor Engines

❄️ Super-cold liquids

Starship’s engines burn two liquids kept very cold: liquid methane (a kind of natural gas) and liquid oxygen. The oxygen is what the methane burns with, because there is no air in space.

🔥 The Raptor engine

Those engines are called Raptor engines. SpaceX says the booster uses 33 of them, and the ship uses 6 (three for landing and flying in air, and three special ones for the emptiness of space).

🪐 A Mars-friendly fuel

Methane is a useful choice for a Mars ship. One day, engineers hope, methane could be made on Mars from the planet’s carbon-dioxide air and hydrogen from water — so a ship might refuel for the trip home. That is a plan, not something already happening.

🛡️ Coming Back: Heat, Drag, Flip, Burn

🧱 The heat shield

When a ship falls back into an atmosphere, air slams into it so hard that it gets extremely hot. Starship is designed with many small heat-shield tiles on its belly, to protect the steel body.

🌬️ Drag is a helper

Drag is the push of air against a moving object. Falling belly-first makes a big area face the wind, so drag can slow the ship a lot — like a skydiver lying flat.

🤸 The belly flop and the flip

On the way down, Starship is designed to fall almost sideways (the “belly flop”) to use that drag. Near the ground it must flip to stand on its tail, then fire engines in a landing burn for a soft touchdown.

🧪 Tested on Earth, planned for Mars

SpaceX has been flight-testing Starship from Texas on Earth. The belly-flop idea is part of those tests. No Starship has flown to Mars or landed there. A Mars landing is a future goal.

🪐 Why Mars Is Different

🪶 Gravity: about 38%

On Mars, gravity is only about 38 percent as strong as on Earth (NASA: 3.73 versus 9.82 meters per second squared). You would weigh less, and a rocket needs less thrust to hover — but falling still speeds you up!

🌬️ Air: about 1% as thick

Mars’s air is very thin — only about 1 percent as thick as Earth’s. That means drag cannot slow a ship as much as it does on Earth. A heat shield is still needed, and engines must do more of the last slowing-down.

📅 A long trip

A typical one-way trip from Earth to Mars takes about 6 to 9 months. The two planets line up well for a launch only about every 26 months. Miss a window, and you wait a long time for the next one!

⛽ Fill up in space (a plan)

To send a lot of cargo all the way to Mars, SpaceX plans for Starship to be refueled in Earth orbit by tanker ships first. That is also still a plan.

🏁 Your Mars Landing Badge

👪 For Grown-ups

What this teaches

  • Rocket basics: Newton’s third law, thrust versus weight, two-stage design, methane/oxygen propellant, and the Raptor engine at a kid level.
  • Reentry ideas: atmospheric drag, a belly-first attitude, a heat shield, a flip, and a landing burn.
  • Why Mars is different: weaker gravity, a much thinner atmosphere, long transit times, and infrequent launch windows.
  • A 2D landing game that uses Mars-like gravity, a high-drag “belly flop” phase, then flip-and-burn with limited fuel.

Level and time

Written for a young 8-year-old who reads ahead (about 3rd–5th grade language, with a few real physics words). About 15–25 minutes, longer if the landing game is replayed.

Accuracy notes and sources

  • Vehicle (SpaceX Starship page, checked November 2026): two-stage fully reusable system; Super Heavy ~72 m, 33 Raptor engines, published thrust 8,240 tf / 18.1 million lbf; Ship ~52 m, 6 engines (3 sea-level Raptor + 3 RVac); stack height 124 m / 407 ft, diameter 9 m; propellants are sub-cooled liquid methane and liquid oxygen. The lesson uses “about” for sizes and skips reciting thrust in the kid cards, because published figures change with engine versions.
  • Not yet at Mars: SpaceX describes Starship as designed to carry crew and cargo to Earth orbit, the Moon, Mars and beyond. Cargo flights to the Martian surface are listed as starting no earlier than 2028. The lesson states clearly that Starship has not flown to or landed on Mars. Earth test flights are mentioned only in general; we do not quote a flight-by-flight scorecard, which changes quickly.
  • Landing profile: Public SpaceX descriptions and Earth test coverage describe a belly-first reentry using heat-shield tiles, then a flip and landing burn. SpaceX’s Mars page says the vehicle would enter Mars’s atmosphere at 7.5 km/s and decelerate aerodynamically; a thin atmosphere still requires retropropulsion for touchdown (NASA EDL discussions). The game is a simplified, sped-up 2D practice, not a simulator of real entry speeds or altitudes.
  • Mars numbers (NASA Mars Fact Sheet): mean surface gravity 3.73 m/s² vs Earth’s 9.82 (ratio 0.380, “about 38%”). Surface pressure 6.36 mb (varies ~4.0–8.7) vs Earth’s ~1013 mb, or about 0.6% of Earth’s pressure. Surface density ~0.016 kg/m³ vs ~1.2 on Earth, around 1%. The lesson says “about 1 percent as thick.” Atmosphere is mostly carbon dioxide.
  • Trip and windows: Typical chemical, low-energy transfers are about 6–9 months (common NASA/education figure). Faster trajectories are studied but are not the usual baseline. Earth–Mars synodic period is 779.94 days, about 26 months (NASA fact sheet).
  • On-orbit refilling and Mars-made methane: described by SpaceX as part of the plan (tanker ships in low Earth orbit). Making methane on Mars via the Sabatier reaction is a well-known engineering idea; the lesson marks it as a hope/plan, not a current operation.
  • Left out / hedged: exact changing thrust and propellant-load figures in kid text; tower “catch” hardware (an Earth recovery plan, easy to confuse with a Mars landing); payload “100+ tonnes” (true on the SpaceX page, but easy to over-claim); any implication that a soft Mars landing has already been done.

What was simplified

The landing game uses Mars gravity (3.71 m/s²) and a thin-air drag model, but distances, speeds, and the flop-to-flip height are packed into a short playable scene. Mass is treated as nearly constant. There is no orbital mechanics in the game. Easy mode is more forgiving (wider pad, more fuel, a slightly higher safe speed).

Follow-up

Jump in the yard or on a trampoline, then ask: “If Mars gravity is only 38% of Earth’s, about how much higher would that jump go?” (About two and a half times.) Together, look up the next Earth–Mars launch window on a NASA site, or watch a parent-approved rocket launch and point out booster versus ship.
