🎬 How They Drew The Aristocats 🐱

How a 1970 cartoon movie was made by hand, one drawing at a time

How do you make a drawing MOVE?

A drawing on paper can’t move. It just sits there. So how did artists make cartoon cats run, jump, and dance? Let’s find out!

That jump was 24 different drawings, shown in just one second! That’s the secret of cartoons.

In 1970, Disney made a cartoon movie called The Aristocats. Every cat in it was drawn by hand, with a pencil, on paper. Let’s see how they did it!

🎬 The Movie

🐱 A Movie About Cats

The Aristocats is a cartoon movie about a mother cat and her three kittens in Paris, France, long ago. They get lost, and a friendly alley cat helps them get home.

📅 Made in 1970

It was made by Walt Disney Productions. It came to movie theaters in December 1970. That’s more than 50 years ago!

👍 Walt Said Yes

Walt Disney saw early drawings and said yes to the idea. That was shortly before he died, in December 1966.

🎬 His Team Carried On

It was the first Disney cartoon movie made completely after Walt died. The director was Wolfgang Reitherman. His friends called him “Woolie.”

👴 The “Nine Old Men”

Walt’s top animators had a funny nickname: the “Nine Old Men.” Several of them worked on this movie. Woolie, the director, was one of them too!

✏️ A Giant Job

The studio said about 250 people worked on it for almost 4 years. They made about 325,000 drawings!

🚫 No Stripes, Please!

The alley cat hero was first going to have tabby stripes. But the director remembered how hard the tiger’s stripes were to animate in The Jungle Book. Stripes would have to be drawn on every single drawing! So they left them off.

👀 How Drawings Move

👀 A Trick on Your Eyes

A cartoon is lots of still pictures, shown very fast, one after another. Your eyes and brain blend them together. So it looks like they are moving!

🎞️ 24 Frames a Second

Movie film shows 24 pictures every second. Each picture is called a frame.

✌️ Drawing “On Twos”

24 drawings for every second is a LOT! So animators often showed each drawing for 2 frames. That’s 12 drawings a second. They call it drawing “on twos.”

⚡ “On Ones” for Fast Moves

For a quick move, twos can look too slow. So animators drew a new picture for every frame. That’s called “on ones.”

✏️ Draw the In-Between

🔑 Key Poses

A top animator drew the most important poses first, like the start and end of a move. These are called keys.

➰ The In-Betweens

Then helpers called in-betweeners drew the drawings in the middle, so the move looks smooth.

🧅 Onion Skin

Animation paper is thin. Artists could see the drawing underneath, like the thin skin of an onion! That helped them draw the next pose. Try the 🧅 button on the flipbook.

🛠️ How They Made It, Step by Step

📋 1. Story First

Story artists drew the whole movie like a giant comic strip, called a storyboard. It helped everyone plan each scene.

🎤 2. Voices Next

Actors recorded the voices before the drawing began. Then the animators made the mouths match the words. In this movie, the cats were even based on their voice actors’ personalities!

🐈 3. Designing the Cats

An artist named Ken Anderson spent about 18 months working out how the characters should look.

🔑 4. Key Poses

Top animators drew the key poses. Four famous ones led the animation: Milt Kahl, Frank Thomas, Ollie Johnston, and John Lounsbery.

🎞️ 5. Pencil Test

The rough pencil drawings were filmed in black and white. Animators watched the test to check the movement before anything was finished.

🧹 6. Clean-Up and In-Betweens

Clean-up artists traced neat lines over the rough drawings. In-betweeners drew the drawings in the middle.

📠 7. The Copy Machine

Long ago, artists traced every line onto clear plastic with ink, by hand. But by this movie, a Xerox copy machine printed the drawings right onto clear sheets called cels. Disney first did this for a whole movie with One Hundred and One Dalmatians, in 1961.

✏️ A Sketchy Look

The copied lines look a bit sketchy, like pencil lines. That’s why cartoons from this time have black, scribbly-looking outlines. Look for them when you watch!

🎨 8. Paint on the Back

Painters painted the colors on the back of each cel. That way, the black lines stay neat on top. The studio mixed its own paints, about 200 colors for this movie!

🖌️ 9. Backgrounds

Background artists painted the streets, rooms, and skies with thin, watery paint. One of them, Al Dempster, had spent a month painting in Paris. The studio said they made about 900 backgrounds!

📷 10. Click, Click, Click

Each cel was laid on its background, with glass on top to hold it flat. A special camera took one picture at a time: one frame, then the next, then the next.

🎵 11. Music and Sound

Songs, music, and sound effects were added. Then the movie went to theaters, just before Christmas, 1970!

      🎞️ Fill Your Film Strip

      👪 For Grown-ups

      What this teaches

      • How hand-drawn (cel) animation works: persistence of vision, 24 frames per second, animating “on twos” versus “on ones,” key poses and in-betweens, onion-skinning.
      • The production pipeline of a late-1960s Disney feature: storyboard, voice recording, character design, key animation, pencil test, clean-up and in-betweening, xerographic transfer to cels, painting on the reverse of the cel, background painting, and frame-by-frame photography on an animation (rostrum) camera.
      • Background on The Aristocats (Walt Disney Productions, US release 24 December 1970; premiere 11 December 1970), directed by Wolfgang Reitherman.

      Level and time

      Written for a young 8-year-old (short sentences, one idea per card), with read-aloud on every card. About 15–20 minutes; easy to split (the movie and how drawings move; then the activities, quiz, and film strip).

      Copyright note

      No images, frames, or character designs from the film are used. “Pip” and every drawing here are original SVG art made for this page; the film is mentioned by name only. A great follow-up is to watch the film together and look for the things in this lesson.

      Accuracy notes and sources

      • Film facts (Walt Disney Productions; Reitherman directing and co-producing with Winston Hibler; Walt approving the project from Ken Anderson’s preliminary sketches shortly before his death on 15 December 1966; animation starting after The Jungle Book was finished; Anderson’s 18 months of character design; characters patterned on the voice actors; O’Malley’s tabby stripes dropped because of the trouble animating Shere Khan; Maurice Chevalier singing the title song; Robin Hood (1973) reusing dance animation from this film) are from Wikipedia’s articles on The Aristocats and Robin Hood. The stripes story is also discussed by Disney animator Andreas Deja on his blog.
      • “First made completely after Walt’s death” comes from the studio’s production notes (reprinted by Blu-ray.com). The project itself began earlier (as an idea for a live-action TV show, starting in 1962), so the lesson says Walt approved it and it was made after his death.
      • Animators: the AFI Catalog credits Milt Kahl, Frank Thomas, Ollie Johnston, and John Lounsbery as directing animators, and Eric Larson as a character animator; Reitherman was also one of the Nine Old Men. Wikipedia says “five” of the Nine Old Men worked on it; because counts differ depending on whether Reitherman is included, the lesson says “several.”
      • Production numbers (about 250 people, nearly four years, about 325,000 drawings, about 900 backgrounds, about 200 studio-mixed colors) are from the studio’s production notes, not independent sources, so they are given as “about” and attributed to the studio.
      • Xerography: used for The Aristocats per the production notes and Deja; Ub Iwerks adapted it for Disney; One Hundred and One Dalmatians (1961) was the first feature to use it throughout (Wikipedia, Traditional animation).
      • Backgrounds: in a 1972 interview (posted on The Animation Guild blog), background artist Al Dempster says they painted with “thin, wash-type poster color,” and that on The Aristocats a Xerox line was also used on backgrounds. His month painting in Paris is from the production notes.
      • General technique (24 fps, twos and ones, storyboards, voices first, pencil tests, peg holes, painting the reverse of the cel, glass platen, animation camera, the word “cel” from celluloid, digital ink and paint at Disney by The Rescuers Down Under, 1990) is from Wikipedia’s Traditional animation article. In real productions these steps overlapped (backgrounds were painted alongside the animation, for example); the order game shows the usual flow.
      • Left out because we could not confirm them well: the exact running time (sources say 78 or 79 minutes), the production notes’ frame count (it doesn’t match its own running time), and any claim that The Aristocats itself reused older animation.
      • Softened: in the film, the butler drugs the cats and takes them away to get the inheritance; the lesson says only that the cats “get lost.”
