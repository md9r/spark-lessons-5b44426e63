Those Greek words mean something like “To Himself.” Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome, wrote his notebook in Greek and never meant for anyone else to read it. Here is one idea from it, in an English translation:

“No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such.”

— Meditations 10.16, translated by George Long (1862)
🧒 In kid words: Stop just talking about how to be good. Go be good!

👑 Who Was Marcus Aurelius?

👶 A Boy in Rome

Marcus was born in the city of Rome in 121 AD, into a rich and important family. He loved learning. Ancient writers say Emperor Hadrian liked the honest boy so much he made a pun on his family name and called him “Verissimus,” which means “most truthful.”

🤝 Picked for the Job

In 138 AD, Hadrian chose Antoninus Pius to be the next emperor, on one condition: Antoninus had to adopt Marcus and a younger boy, Lucius Verus, as his sons. So at about 17, Marcus was on the path to becoming emperor.

📚 A Student for Life

Marcus had many teachers. His favorite subject became philosophy, which means “love of wisdom.” A teacher named Junius Rusticus shared with him the lessons of Epictetus, a Stoic teacher who had once been enslaved.

👑👑 Two Emperors at Once

When Antoninus died in 161 AD, Marcus became emperor. He surprised everyone by making his adopted brother Lucius Verus co-emperor. Rome had two emperors ruling side by side for the first time! Lucius died in 169 AD, and then Marcus ruled alone.

⭐ One of the “Five Good Emperors”

Much later, historians grouped five emperors in a row as the “Five Good Emperors”: Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius. They were remembered for ruling fairly well and choosing their next ruler carefully. Marcus was the last of the five.

🦉 The Philosopher King

Long before, the Greek thinker Plato wrote that the best ruler would be a philosopher. Marcus is often called the closest Rome ever came to a “philosopher king.” He tried to rule with wisdom, fairness, and self-control, though he was a human who made mistakes too.

📓 A Secret Notebook

Marcus kept a private notebook, written in Greek, the language of philosophy in his time (he also spoke Latin). He wrote reminders to himself. Some parts were written while he was with his army along the Danube River. Today we call it the Meditations.

🏇 A Statue That Survived

A huge bronze statue of Marcus on a horse still exists in Rome. Many bronze statues of emperors were melted down over the centuries, but people long believed this one showed Constantine, the first Christian emperor, and that probably helped save it! The original is now kept indoors at the Capitoline Museums.

🦉 What Is a Stoic?

🏛️ A Porch Full of Thinkers

Around 300 BC, a teacher named Zeno taught in Athens at a painted porch called the Stoa. His followers were nicknamed Stoics, “porch people”! Marcus lived about 450 years later, but he loved their ideas.

✋ The Big Stoic Idea

Epictetus taught (here in our own words): some things are up to us, and some things are not. Up to us: our choices, our effort, and how we treat people. Not up to us: the weather, other people's choices, and lots of what happens. Spend your energy on the first group!

💭 It's How You Think About It

Stoics believed that our thoughts about an event often upset us more than the event itself. Rain on game day isn't “terrible” by itself. You can choose to think, “Okay, what can I do now?”

😌 Not “No Feelings”

Today “stoic” can mean hiding your feelings, but the Stoics didn't mean never feel sad. They meant: notice your feelings, then let good thinking steer what you do, not anger or fear.

📓 Inside the Meditations

The Meditations is 12 short “books” (really chapters) of notes. Marcus wrote them for himself, not for us! Here are a few famous lines, in a well-known English translation by George Long (1862), with a kid-words version.

“The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.”

— Meditations 6.6, translated by George Long
🧒 In kid words: If someone is mean to you, the best answer is to not become mean too.

“If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it.”

— Meditations 8.47, translated by George Long
🧒 In kid words: Often what upsets us most is the story we tell ourselves about a thing, and we can change that story.

🏕️ Written in an Army Camp

Old copies of the notebook include little notes that say where parts were written: one says “among the Quadi, by the Granua” (a river in today's Slovakia), and another says “at Carnuntum,” a Roman army base on the Danube. So the emperor was writing about patience and kindness in a tent near the frontier!

🌅 His Morning Reminder (in our words)

Near the start of Book 2, Marcus reminds himself that today he will probably meet people who are rude or ungrateful. Instead of getting angry, he tells himself they act that way because they don't know better, and that people are made to work together, like hands and feet. (This is our summary, not his exact words.)

🌧️ Hard Times for Rome

Marcus's years as emperor were not peaceful. These are true and serious events, told gently.

🤒 The Antonine Plague

Around 165–166 AD, soldiers coming home from a war in the east brought back a terrible sickness. It spread across the empire for years, and very many people died. Scientists today think it may have been smallpox, a disease that vaccines have now wiped out. Marcus had to keep the empire going through all of it.

⚔️ Wars on the Danube

Groups of people from north of the Danube River, like the Marcomanni and Quadi, crossed into Roman lands. Marcus spent much of his last 12 or so years far from Rome, with his armies on the frontier. He didn't love war, but he believed defending the empire was his duty. Ancient writers say he even sold palace treasures to pay for it rather than raise taxes.

🕯️ The End of His Life

Marcus died in 180 AD, while still with his army near the Danube, probably at Vindobona (today's Vienna) or Sirmium; old writers don't agree which. He was 58.

👦 A Disappointing Son

The four emperors before Marcus had picked grown men as their next rulers. Marcus instead passed power to his own son, Commodus. Commodus turned out to be a poor ruler who cared more about showing off than governing well, and his reign ended badly in 192 AD. Even a wise person can't control everything, including other people's choices. Marcus would have called that a hard Stoic lesson.

💡 Why Do People Still Read Him?

The Meditations is one of the only books we have where a ruler of a mighty empire wrote honestly to himself about his worries and how to be good. It's been printed in many languages, and today students, soldiers, athletes, doctors, business leaders, and kids read it for help staying calm, brave, and kind. Its ideas, like focusing on what's up to you, are still used by people who teach others how to handle worry.

👪 For Grown-ups

What this teaches

  • Who Marcus Aurelius was (born 121 AD; emperor 161–180 AD; co-ruler with Lucius Verus until 169; last of the “Five Good Emperors”) and what the Roman Empire looked like around 170 AD.
  • The core Stoic idea of what is and isn't in our control (from Epictetus), the four Stoic virtues, and the Meditations as a private notebook in Greek.
  • Honest history: the Antonine Plague, the Marcomannic Wars, and the succession of Commodus, told without graphic detail.

Grade band

About grades 3–5, a bit above for strong readers. Read-aloud supports every card.

Time

About 30–40 minutes; easy to split (life, map and timeline, then Stoic ideas, sorting game, notebook, and quiz).

Accuracy notes

  • Quotations are from George Long's public-domain 1862 translation (6.6, 8.47, 10.16). The Book 2 “morning reminder” is a labeled paraphrase. The Epictetus idea is paraphrased (Handbook/Enchiridion, ch. 1).
  • The title “To Himself” comes from the manuscript tradition; Marcus may not have titled it. The place notes (“among the Quadi, by the Granua”; “at Carnuntum”) appear at the breaks after Books 1 and 2 in the manuscripts (some editions print them as headings of Books 2 and 3).
  • The “Verissimus” nickname and the sale of palace goods come from ancient sources (Cassius Dio and the less reliable Historia Augusta), so they're attributed to “ancient writers.”
  • The plague's identity (probably smallpox) and its death toll are debated. Marcus's place of death is disputed (Vindobona or Sirmium).
  • Persecutions of Christians took place in some provinces during his reign (e.g., St. Justin Martyr in Rome, c. 165, and the martyrs of Lyon, 177). This isn't covered in the kids' cards but is worth knowing for discussion.
  • The map is a simplified sketch, not a precise historical boundary.

Follow-up

Keep a 3-line “Meditations” journal for a week at bedtime: (1) one thing that went well, (2) one thing that wasn't up to me, (3) one thing I'll try to do better tomorrow.
