What if you fell asleep in a magic forest… and woke up with a DONKEY’S HEAD?

Fairies. A tricky sprite. A magic flower. And lots and lots of silly mix-ups! This is one of the funniest plays ever written.

This story is called A Midsummer Night’s Dream. It was written by William Shakespeare more than 400 years ago.

Midsummer means the middle of summer. In old stories, it was a time when magic was in the air!

✒️ Who Was Shakespeare?

✒️ A Famous Writer

William Shakespeare lived in England more than 400 years ago. Many people call him the greatest writer in the English language.

🏡 Where He Was Born

He was born in 1564 in a small town called Stratford-upon-Avon.

👑 A Royal Link

When Shakespeare was born, the queen of England was Elizabeth I. She was Henry VIII’s daughter!

🎭 Actor and Writer

He moved to London. He acted in plays, and he wrote them too.

📚 So Many Plays

He wrote more than 35 plays, plus lots of poems. Some plays are funny. Some are sad.

🏟️ The Globe Theatre

In 1599, his acting group built the Globe Theatre in London. It was round, and open to the sky!

🧍 Standing Room

For just a penny, people could stand right in front of the stage to watch. Plays happened in the afternoon, in daylight.

😂 A Comedy

He wrote A Midsummer Night’s Dream around 1595. It is a comedy: a funny play with a happy ending.

🕯️ His Plays Live On

Shakespeare died in 1616. But people still act out his plays all over the world today!

🏛️ The Story, Part 1: In Athens

🏛️ Long Ago in Athens

The story starts in Athens, a city in Greece, long ago. Duke Theseus is getting married to Queen Hippolyta.

💞 Hermia and Lysander

Hermia loves Lysander. And Lysander loves Hermia. So far, so good!

💔 Helena and Demetrius

Helena loves Demetrius. But Demetrius wants to marry Hermia! Poor Helena.

😠 A Very Strict Father

Hermia’s father wants her to marry Demetrius, not Lysander. He was very strict, and the law of Athens was harsh.

🌲 Run to the Forest!

So Hermia and Lysander run away into the forest at night. Demetrius follows them. And Helena follows Demetrius!

🧚 The Story, Part 2: The Fairies

The forest is full of fairies. People can’t see them, but the fairies can see the people!

👑 The Fairy King and Queen

Oberon is the king of the fairies. Titania is the queen.

😤 A Big Argument

Oberon and Titania are having a big argument. They both want to look after the same little boy.

😜 Meet Puck

Puck is Oberon’s helper. He is a fast, tricky fairy who loves playing jokes. His other name is Robin Goodfellow.

🌸 A Magic Flower

Oberon sends Puck to find a magic flower called love-in-idleness.

👀 How the Magic Works

Put the flower’s juice on someone’s eyes while they sleep. When they wake up, they will love the first creature they see!

🙃 The Big Mix-Up

Oberon tells Puck to help Helena. But Puck puts the juice on the wrong sleeping man! Soon, nobody loves the right person.

🎭 The Story, Part 3: The Actors

🔨 Six Workmen

Six workmen from Athens want to put on a play for the Duke’s wedding. There is a carpenter, a weaver, a tailor, and more.

🙋 Nick Bottom

Bottom the weaver is VERY excited. He wants to play every part, even the lion!

🌲 Practice in the Woods

The actors go into the forest to practice their play, where nobody can see them. But the fairies can!

🫏 A Donkey Head!

Puck plays a trick. He gives Bottom a donkey’s head! Bottom’s friends run away scared. Bottom has no idea what happened.

😍 Titania Wakes Up

Oberon put the flower juice on Titania’s eyes. She wakes up, sees Bottom with his donkey head, and falls in love with him!

🌾 Hay, Please!

Titania’s fairies treat Bottom like a king. They scratch his hairy head. And he asks for hay and oats to munch!

💒 The Story, Part 4: A Happy Ending

🌿 All Fixed!

Puck uses a cure to fix the mix-up. Lysander loves Hermia again. And Demetrius now loves Helena!

🤝 Friends Again

Oberon wakes Titania from the spell. The fairy king and queen are friends again. Puck takes off Bottom’s donkey head.

💭 Was It a Dream?

Everyone wakes up in the morning. They all feel like they had a strange dream!

💒 Three Weddings

The Duke says Hermia can marry Lysander. So there are three weddings: Theseus and Hippolyta, Hermia and Lysander, and Helena and Demetrius.

🎪 The Silliest Play

Then the workmen put on their play, Pyramus and Thisbe. It is supposed to be sad, but they act it so badly that everyone laughs!

✨ Goodnight, Fairies

At night, the fairies bless the happy couples. Then Puck says goodbye to the audience.

There are three groups in this play: lords and lovers from Athens, fairies from the forest, and the actors, who are Athens workmen putting on a play. Which group does each character belong to?

    👪 For Grown-ups

    What this teaches

    • Who Shakespeare was (1564–1616; Stratford-upon-Avon; actor and playwright; the Globe, 1599), with a link to the Henry VIII lesson (Elizabeth I was on the throne when he was born).
    • The plot of A Midsummer Night’s Dream (c. 1595–96) in four parts, its three groups of characters, and how a comedy works (mix-ups that are put right, ending in weddings).
    • Real lines from the play with plain-English versions, plus Early Modern English words (thou, thy, wherefore, hither, whither, anon, methinks), including the classic “wherefore = why” trap.
    • Story sequencing, and the idea of a play-within-a-play (Pyramus and Thisbe).

    Level and time

    Written for a young 8-year-old (short sentences, one idea per card), with read-aloud on every card. About 15–20 minutes; easy to split into two sittings (Shakespeare and the story; then the activities, quiz, and fairy ring).

    What was softened (for you to discuss if and when you choose)

    • Egeus’s threat (Act 1, Scene 1): Hermia’s father invokes Athenian law, under which she must marry Demetrius or face death; Theseus adds the option of life as a nun. The lesson says only that her father “was very strict, and the law of Athens was harsh.”
    • The fairy quarrel: the boy is a “changeling” child whose mother (a devotee of Titania) died giving birth; Oberon enchants Titania partly to get the boy from her, and she is humiliated while under the spell. The lesson says only that they “both want to look after the same little boy.”
    • The love potion and consent: the enchanted lovers (and Titania with Bottom) don’t choose freely. Demetrius stays enchanted at the end. You may want to talk about this with an older child.
    • Rough words and threats: Demetrius threatens Helena in the wood (Act 2, Scene 1); the lovers hurl insults in Act 3, Scene 2 (“puppet,” “acorn,” and so on); Bottom’s “ass” jokes. These are left out.
    • Pyramus and Thisbe: in the play-within-the-play both lovers stab themselves (comically, in Act 5, Scene 1). The lesson calls it a pretend sad ending acted so badly that everyone laughs, and mentions only Bottom’s “die, die, die, die, die.”

    Accuracy notes

    • Every Shakespeare line was checked against the full text (Project Gutenberg edition). Act and scene numbers use standard modern numbering. Puck’s closing speech (“If we shadows have offended…”) is printed at the end of Act 5, Scene 1; some editions label it an Epilogue. The Juliet line is from Romeo and Juliet, Act 2, Scene 2.
    • Shakespeare was baptized on 26 April 1564 (his birthday is traditionally given as 23 April); he died on 23 April 1616. The Globe was built in 1599, burned in 1613 during a performance of Henry VIII (also called All Is True) when a stage cannon set the thatch alight, and was rebuilt in 1614. The modern reconstruction, Shakespeare’s Globe, opened in 1997.
    • The workmen (“rude mechanicals”) are Peter Quince (carpenter), Nick Bottom (weaver), Francis Flute (bellows-mender), Tom Snout (tinker), Snug (joiner), and Robin Starveling (tailor). Love-in-idleness is the wild pansy (heartsease). The cure for Titania is named “Dian’s bud.”
    • In Shakespeare’s England, female roles were played by boys; women did not act on the public stage until the 1660s. Uranus’s moons Oberon, Titania, and Puck are named after these characters. Mendelssohn’s incidental music (including the “Wedding March”) dates from 1842.

    Follow-up activity

    Act out a scene: try the workmen handing out parts (Act 1, Scene 2, where Bottom wants to play the lion too) or the Pyramus and Thisbe play, with someone holding up fingers as the Wall’s “chink.” Make Bottom’s donkey mask: cut long ears from paper, tape them to a paper plate with eye holes, and draw a big donkey nose. Then ask: what would you do if you woke up with a donkey head?
