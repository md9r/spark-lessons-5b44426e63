👋 Before We Start

🤔 Why Learn About Wars?

Wars are some of the biggest events in history. They changed maps, created new countries, and changed how people live. Learning about them helps us understand the world today — and how to keep peace.

💙 A Gentle Reminder

Wars are very sad. In every war, many people were hurt or died, and many families lost their homes. It's okay to feel sad or have questions. Talking with a grown-up about it is a great idea.

🔍 Five Big Questions

For each war, we'll ask: Why did it start? Who were the key people? What was the turning point? How did it end? And what changed afterward?

🐴 Legend or History? The Trojan War

You might hear about the Trojan War and the giant wooden horse. That story comes from very old Greek poems by a poet named Homer. There really was an ancient city called Troy, in what is now Turkey, but historians aren't sure the war happened the way the poems tell it. So we call it a legend, not history we can prove.

✅ WWII Quick Quiz

🕊️ How Wars End — and the Peacemakers

📜 Treaties

A treaty is a written agreement between countries. The Treaty of Paris (1783) ended the American Revolution. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) ended World War I.

🤝 Ceasefires and Surrenders

An armistice or ceasefire means both sides agree to stop fighting. A surrender is when one side gives up, like when General Lee surrendered to General Grant in 1865.

🌐 The United Nations

After World War II, countries started the United Nations in 1945 so they could talk out problems instead of fighting. Today 193 countries are members.

⚖️ A Fair Peace Lasts Longer

The Treaty of Versailles was very harsh on Germany. Many historians think the anger it caused helped lead to World War II. Peacemakers learned that a fair peace is more likely to last.

🏅 Peacemakers

Diplomats are people whose job is to help countries solve problems by talking. Every year, the Nobel Peace Prize honors people who work for peace.

💛 Enemies Can Become Friends

Many countries that once fought are now close friends — like the United States and Britain, France and Germany, and the United States and Japan.

👪 For Grown-ups

What this teaches

  • Eight major wars from ancient Rome to the Cold War (including the War of 1812), using one simple frame: causes, key people, turning points, endings, and what changed.
  • A World War II deep dive: the road to war, Europe, the Pacific, the home front, heroes and helpers, the Holocaust, science and technology, and how the world changed — with a step-by-step map, a code-breaking puzzle, and a short quiz.
  • Chronology (how far apart events are) with a zoomable timeline, and geography with a simple world map.
  • Cause and effect, and how wars end: treaties, ceasefires, surrenders, and the United Nations.

Grade band

About grades 3–5. Read-aloud helps younger readers.

Sensitive topics

Deaths and suffering are mentioned honestly but without graphic detail. Please preview: slavery (Civil War); the loss of Native lands (War of 1812); and in the WWII deep dive, the Holocaust tab (Anne Frank, about six million Jewish people and millions of others killed — no images or graphic details), the atomic bombs, and the forced removal of Japanese Americans to camps (Home Front tab). Being nearby for questions is a good idea.

Time

About 60–90 minutes for everything: roughly 40–60 minutes for the eight wars and games, plus 25–35 minutes for the WWII deep dive. Best split into three or four sittings (for example, one era at a time, with WWII on its own day).

Follow-up activity

Make a cause-and-change chain. Pick one war and draw three linked boxes: "Why it started" → "Turning point" → "What changed." If you can, talk with a grandparent, neighbor, or veteran about what they remember or learned about a war from their lifetime.

WWII extra: plant a small "victory garden" pot (beans or herbs) and talk about why families grew their own food during the war.
